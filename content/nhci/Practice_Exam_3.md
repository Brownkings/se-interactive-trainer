# Suggested Solutions

## Section A Solutions

### Question 1: Designing for Sensual Experience
* **1.1 Sensual Experience**:
  * A sensual experience refers to the way people perceive and interact with a product, system, or environment through their senses—sight, sound, touch, taste, and smell. (5)
* **1.2 Subjective Constraint**:
  * Experiences are subjective, internal, and depend on the user's emotional state, personal history, memories, and immediate context. Therefore, designers cannot program or force a specific experience; they can only design physical features that increase the likelihood of evoking that experience. (4)
* **1.3 Proposed Features (3 Marks each)**:
  * *Visual Design (for Calmness)*: Use a clean, minimalist layout with a soothing, harmonious color palette (e.g., soft blues, warm grays) and ample white space to reduce visual clutter and cognitive overload.
  * *Interaction Design (for Professionalism)*: Implement a responsive, lag-free navigation bar with smooth micro-animations on hover, giving clear feedback and demonstrating high-quality system engineering.

### Question 2: Process of Interaction Design
* **2.1 Four Basic Activities (2 Marks each)**:
  * *Establishing Requirements*: Discovering user needs, capabilities, and system constraints.
  * *Designing Alternatives*: Generating potential conceptual models and interface designs.
  * *Prototyping*: Creating interactive mockups to explore and test design ideas.
  * *Evaluating*: Testing the interface with users or experts to check usability.
* **2.2 Integrated Evaluation**:
  * If evaluation is left until the end, finding major conceptual or design flaws would require rewriting massive amounts of code, which is expensive and slow. Integrating evaluation at each step (e.g., evaluating wireframes, then interactive mockups, then code) allows errors to be caught early when they are cheap to fix, ensuring the system remains user-centric. (7)

### Question 3: Evaluation in Natural Settings
* **3.1 Field Study**:
  * A field study is a method of evaluating how a product is used by people in their normal, day-to-day settings (natural environments) with little or no intervention from evaluators. (4)
* **3.2 Field Studies vs. Lab Settings**:
  * *Advantages (Any three, 2 Marks each)*:
    * High ecological validity: Shows how the product is used in real-world contexts.
    * Uncovers unexpected usage patterns, workarounds, or environmental distractions.
    * Captures long-term user habits and emotional adoption.
  * *Disadvantages (Any two, 2 Marks each)*:
    * Evaluators have little to no control over user activities or external distractions.
    * Difficult to replicate test conditions or capture precise quantitative performance logs.
* **3.3 Participant Size**:
  * Jakob Nielsen's empirical research showed that testing with 5 users uncovers about 85% of usability issues. Adding more users yields diminishing returns, as they tend to repeat the same errors. Hence, 5 to 12 users is the most cost-effective range for usability tests. (6)
