# Chapter 5: Evaluation and Inspections

## What You Must Learn and Know

### 1. Types of Evaluation
Evaluation assesses the usability and user experience of a product. It is classified into two types:
* **Formative Evaluation**: Done during the design and prototyping phases to identify usability problems and shape/improve the design.
* **Summative Evaluation**: Done after development is complete to assess overall usability, performance, and user satisfaction against benchmarks.

### 2. Evaluation Settings
Evaluations are conducted in three categories of settings:
1. **Controlled Settings involving Users**: (e.g., Usability Laboratories, Living Labs) to test hypotheses and measure user behavior with minimized distractions.
2. **Natural Settings involving Users**: (e.g., Field Studies) to see how products are used in real-world contexts with little or no control.
3. **Any Settings not involving Users**: (e.g., Expert Inspections) where experts critique, predict, and model interfaces.

### 3. Usability Testing
* **Definition**: Evaluating a product by testing it on representative users performing predefined tasks in a controlled setting.
* **Quantitative Measures**:
  * Time to complete a task.
  * Number and type of errors per task.
  * Number of navigations to help/manuals.
  * Success rate (percentage of users completing task).
* **Participant Count**: **5 to 12 users** is generally acceptable to find the majority of usability problems.

### 4. Usability Inspection: Heuristic Evaluation
* **Heuristic Evaluation** (developed by Jakob Nielsen) is an inspection method where usability experts independently evaluate whether user interface elements conform to established usability principles (heuristics).
* **Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics**:
  1. *Visibility of system status*: Keep users informed with timely feedback.
  2. *Match between system and real world*: Speak the user's language, use familiar words/phrases.
  3. *User control and freedom*: Support Undo, Redo, and provide clear emergency exits.
  4. *Consistency and standards*: Avoid confusing users with different words meaning the same thing.
  5. *Error prevention*: Design interfaces that prevent errors before they occur.
  6. *Recognition rather than recall*: Minimize memory load; make options and instructions visible.
  7. *Flexibility and efficiency of use*: Provide accelerators for expert users.
  8. *Aesthetic and minimalist design*: Dialogues should not contain irrelevant info.
  9. *Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors*: Plain-language error messages suggesting solutions.
  10. *Help and documentation*: Easily searchable and task-focused documentation.
* **Three Stages of Heuristic Evaluation**:
  1. *Briefing session*: Experts are told the goals and tasks.
  2. *Evaluation period*: Each expert spends 1-2 hours independently inspecting the interface (at least two passes).
  3. *Debriefing session*: Experts aggregate findings, prioritize problems, and discuss solutions.

### 5. Walkthroughs
* **Cognitive Walkthrough**: Simulating a user's problem-solving process at each step of an action sequence. It focuses on evaluating designs for **ease of learning** (especially for novice users).
* **Cognitive Walkthrough Questions**:
  * Will the correct action be sufficiently evident to the user?
  * Will the user notice that the correct action is available?
  * Will the user associate and interpret the response from the action correctly?

## Key Terms and Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Formative Evaluation** | Usability evaluation conducted during design to guide improvements. |
| **Summative Evaluation** | Usability evaluation conducted at the end of development to measure success. |
| **Heuristic Evaluation** | An inspection method where experts check an interface against 10 usability rules. |
| **Cognitive Walkthrough** | A walkthrough method simulating user problem-solving to evaluate ease of learning. |
| **Usability Testing** | Testing a prototype on representative users in a controlled environment. |

## Practice Test

### Section A: Multiple-Choice Questions
1. Usability evaluation conducted during the design and prototyping phases to shape the final design is known as:
   a) Summative Evaluation
   b) Formative Evaluation
   c) Heuristic Evaluation
   d) Cognitive Walkthrough

2. What is the recommended number of users to involve in a usability testing study to identify most usability problems?
   a) 1 to 2
   b) 5 to 12
   c) 50 to 100
   d) At least 1000

3. Which usability heuristic is violated if a system deletes a student's booking without asking for confirmation or offering an undo option?
   a) User control and freedom
   b) Consistency and standards
   c) Flexibility and efficiency
   d) Aesthetic and minimalist design

4. What is the primary focus of a Cognitive Walkthrough?
   a) Measuring page load performance
   b) Evaluating a design for ease of learning
   c) Database query optimization
   d) Establishing project budgets

### Section B: Short-Answer & Scenario Questions
5. Differentiate between Formative and Summative Evaluation.
6. List and briefly explain five of Nielsen's usability heuristics.
7. Outline the three stages of conducting a Heuristic Evaluation.

## Answer Key

### Section A Solutions
1. **b) Formative Evaluation**. Formative evaluation is done during design to form and improve the product.
2. **b) 5 to 12**. Jakob Nielsen showed that testing 5 to 12 users is the most cost-effective size for identifying major usability flaws.
3. **a) User control and freedom**. Supporting undo/redo and emergency exits satisfies user control and freedom.
4. **b) Evaluating a design for ease of learning**. Cognitive walkthroughs focus on whether novice users can easily figure out how to perform tasks.

### Section B Solutions
5. **Formative Evaluation** is conducted during design and prototyping (formative stages) to detect usability flaws and improve the interface. **Summative Evaluation** is conducted after the product is completed (summative stage) to measure overall usability, speed, error rates, and user satisfaction against benchmarks.
6. Nielsen's usability heuristics (any five):
   * **Visibility of system status**: Keep users informed with timely feedback (e.g., loading spinner).
   * **Match between system and real world**: Use terms and concepts familiar to the user rather than system-oriented terms.
   * **User control and freedom**: Support undo/redo and easy exit from unwanted states.
   * **Consistency and standards**: Use consistent labels and interactions throughout the system.
   * **Error prevention**: Design screens to prevent mistakes (e.g., date pickers instead of typing dates).
   * **Recognition rather than recall**: Make options visible to reduce memory load.
   * **Flexibility and efficiency**: Accelerators for advanced users.
   * **Aesthetic and minimalist design**: Keep clutter to a minimum.
   * **Help users recover from errors**: Clear error messages with constructive suggestions.
   * **Help and documentation**: Provide easy-to-use search help.
7. The three stages of Heuristic Evaluation:
   * **Briefing session**: Experts are briefed on the system, target user profiles, and tasks to evaluate.
   * **Evaluation period**: Experts independently evaluate the system (taking at least two passes) using the heuristics.
   * **Debriefing session**: Experts meet to discuss, merge, prioritize usability issues, and brainstorm solutions.
