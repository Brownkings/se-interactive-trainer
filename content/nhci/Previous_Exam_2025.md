# Suggested Solutions

## Section A Solutions

### Question 1: Interaction Design Foundations
* **1.1 Interaction Design & UX**:
  * **Interaction Design** is designing interactive products to support the way people communicate and interact in their everyday and working lives. (2)
  * **User Experience** refers to how a product behaves and is used by people in the real world—how they feel about it, their satisfaction, pleasure, etc. (2)
  * Interaction Design is the activity, while User Experience is the outcome. Designers design *for* a good user experience. (1)
* **1.2 Accessibility vs Inclusiveness**:
  * **Accessibility** is the extent to which an interactive product is usable by as many people as possible, particularly disabled people (e.g., screen readers for blind users). (2.5)
  * **Inclusiveness** is making products accommodate the widest possible range of people regardless of disability, education, age, or income (e.g., designing an app that works on low-end smartphones with slow internet). (2.5)
* **1.3 Understanding Context**:
  * It helps designers avoid designing systems based on their own biases. By understanding the context (where, how, and who will use the system), they can design products that match users' actual needs, capabilities, and cognitive constraints. (5)

### Question 2: User-Centered Design Principles
* **2.1 Gould and Lewis Principles (3 Marks each)**:
  * *Early focus on users and tasks*: Observing users, studying the tasks they perform, and involving them directly in the design process from the start.
  * *Empirical measurement*: Using simulations, paper prototypes, and manuals early in development to measure user reaction and performance.
  * *Iterative design*: A cycle of design-test-modify, where usability problems are fixed and re-tested.
* **2.2 Formative vs. Summative Evaluation**:
  * **Formative evaluation** is conducted during design and prototyping to shape and improve the interface. (3)
  * **Summative evaluation** is conducted at the end of development to measure the final system's success, speed, and user satisfaction. (3)

### Question 3: Evaluation & Inspections
* **3.1 Three Stages of Heuristic Evaluation (2 Marks each)**:
  * *Briefing*: Evaluators are told the goals, target users, and tasks to focus on.
  * *Evaluation Period*: Each expert independently inspects the system (taking at least two passes) against usability heuristics.
  * *Debriefing*: Experts meet to aggregate their findings, prioritize usability issues, and suggest fixes.
* **3.2 Nielsen's Heuristics (Any four, 2 Marks each)**:
  * *Visibility of system status*: The system should keep users informed of what is going on through timely feedback.
  * *Match between system and real world*: Speak the user's language with familiar words and concepts.
  * *User control and freedom*: Provide "emergency exits" to leave unwanted states, support undo/redo.
  * *Consistency and standards*: Follow conventions so users don't wonder if different words mean the same thing.
  * *Error prevention*: Design interfaces to prevent errors before they occur.
  * *Recognition rather than recall*: Make objects and options visible to reduce memory load.
  * *Flexibility and efficiency*: Accelerators to help expert users complete tasks quickly.
  * *Aesthetic and minimalist design*: Omit irrelevant information.
  * *Help users recover from errors*: Error messages should be plain, precise, and constructive.
  * *Help and documentation*: Searchable, task-focused documentation.
* **3.3 Cognitive Walkthrough**:
  * *Definition & Focus*: A usability inspection method simulating user problem-solving at each step. It focuses on evaluating the design for **ease of learning**. (3)
  * *Questions (Any two, 1.5 Marks each)*:
    * Will the correct action be sufficiently evident to the user?
    * Will the user notice that the correct action is available?
    * Will the user associate and interpret the response from the action correctly?
